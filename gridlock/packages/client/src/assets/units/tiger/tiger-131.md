# Tiger 131 — Pz.Kpfw. VI Ausf. E (early)

**Designation:** Panzerkampfwagen VI Ausführung E *Tiger* (Sd.Kfz. 181)  
**This vehicle:** Fahrgestell Nr. **250122**, turret number **131**  
**Unit:** schwere Panzer-Abteilung 504, 1. Kompanie, 3. Zug, first tank (platoon commander)  
**Type:** Heavy tank  
**Period for this spec:** February–April 1943 (Tunisia). Early production, not a late-war steel-wheel / *Zimmerit* Tiger.

Tiger 131 is a *specific* tank, not a stand-in for every Tiger I. It was accepted from Henschel in February 1943, shipped to Tunisia with s.Pz.Abt. 504, and captured intact by the British on **24 April 1943** at Point 174 (Gueriat el Atach), west of Medjez el Bab. It is the first Tiger taken complete by the Western Allies, and today the only Tiger I that still runs (The Tank Museum, Bovington).

“131” is a tactical number: 1st company, 3rd platoon, vehicle 1. The army knew it as chassis 250122.

---

## Role

Breakthrough and overwatch heavy tank. Issued only to independent heavy battalions (*schwere Panzer-Abteilungen*), never as a line-company medium. In Tunisia that meant a handful of Tigers stiffening Panzer IIIs and IVs against Churchills, Shermans and 6-pounders.

**What it does well**

- Kills every Allied tank in North Africa from the front at ranges those tanks cannot answer.
- Frontal armour that 75 mm and 6-pdr shot bounce off at normal combat ranges unless they find a weak spot or a freak angle.
- Hydraulic turret traverse and a very accurate 8.8 cm — first-round hits at 1,000–1,500 m are realistic for a trained crew.

**What it does badly**

- Rare, thirsty, and a recovery nightmare. A broken Tiger is often a lost Tiger.
- Tall (~3.0 m to the drum cupola) and wide (3.70 m on combat tracks). Easy to spot, hard to hide, hard to move through olive groves, wadis and mountain passes.
- Early HL 210 + desert dust + Feifel filters: reliability is a feature, not a given.
- Thin roof (25 mm). Artillery airburst and aircraft matter.
- Sides and rear are thick by 1942 standards, not by 1944 standards — and even in 1943 a 6-pdr that gets a side-on shot at close range can kill it. That is how *this* tank died.

### Early / Tunisia features that belong on 131

- **Drum cupola** (cylindrical, vision slits) — not the later cast periscope cupola (that starts ~July 1943, turret ~392).
- Early gun mantlet, **without** the later sight-aperture reinforcement.
- **Feifel** external cyclone air filters on the rear hull (desert dust). Early two-canister style.
- Turret-side **smoke dischargers** (NbK 39), three per side. 131’s top-left discharger was already missing in German photos before capture.
- Rubber-rimmed interleaved wheels, not late steel rims.
- 25 mm turret and hull roof — **not** the 40–45 mm roof from March 1944.
- No *Zimmerit*.
- No factory *Schürzen*.
- Spare track on the lower bow; a step welded to the nose / spare-track bracket (a 131 recognition feature).
- S-mine launchers on the rear hull corners (brackets present).
- Tropical / desert yellow finish; red turret number **131**.

Battle damage that should stay if you model *this* vehicle after 24 April: 6-pdr scar under the gun / into the turret ring, hit on the right turret lifting lug, smashed loader’s hatch.

## Armor

Rolled homogeneous nickel-steel, interleaved-welded plates. The Tiger is a **box**. Almost no slope except the short lower nose. Protection comes from thickness, not angle.

| Zone | Thickness | Angle (from vertical) | Game note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower hull nose | **100 mm** | ~25° | Strong vs 75 mm / 6-pdr |
| Driver’s front plate | **100 mm** | ~9° | Nearly vertical. The plate 6-pdrs dreamed about and usually failed against |
| Glacis (small, steep) | ~60 mm | ~80° | Small target |
| Upper hull / sponson sides | **80 mm** | 0° | Thick, but flat. 6-pdr and 75 mm can punch this at short–medium range with a clean side shot |
| Lower hull sides | **60 mm** | 0° | Weaker than the sponson. Wheels and torsion bars live here |
| Hull rear | **80 mm** | ~8° | |
| Hull roof / floor | **25 mm** | flat | Artillery, bombs, mines |
| Turret front | **100 mm** | ~8–10° | |
| Gun mantlet | **120 mm** typical (locally up to ~200 mm at the boss) | rounded / 0° | Shot trap under the gun is how 131 was stopped |
| Turret sides / rear | **80 mm** | 0° | |
| Turret roof | **25 mm** | nearly flat | |
| Drum cupola | ~50–80 mm ring; **~75 mm** at the vision slits | — | Commander’s head is less protected than the front plate |

**Against 1943 Allied guns (Tunisia)**

- Sherman 75 mm and Churchill 6-pdr: frontal kill is unlikely at combat range. Side / rear / turret-ring / roof shots are the real threat.
- 131 itself proves the point: three 6-pdr hits from Churchills of 48 RTR / supporting fire did not pierce the front plate. One ricocheted under the barrel into the **turret ring**, jammed traverse, wrecked the radio, and the crew bailed.

**Weak spots worth coding**

- Turret ring (the shot that killed *this* tank)
- Underside of the gun / mantlet join (shot trap)
- Drum cupola vision slits
- Driver’s visor and MG ball mount
- 25 mm roof
- Rear engine deck and Feifel plumbing
- Lower side above the wheels
- Interleaved wheels — mobility kill long before a brew-up

## Gun and ammunition — 8.8 cm KwK 36 L/56

Derived from the Flak 18/36. Same ballistics, tank mount, electric fire.

| | |
|---|---|
| Traverse | **360°** hydraulic + hand |
| Elevation | **−6.5° / +17°** (sources vary slightly, −8 / +15 also quoted) |
| Sight | TZF 9b (2.5×, 24°) |
| Ready / stowed | **92** rounds (some Tigers field-modified for more) |
| Coax / bow MG | 2 × 7.92 mm MG 34, ~4,800 rounds |

Hydraulic traverse speed depends on engine rpm — roughly 360° in **25–60 seconds**. Not a snap turret. Still a turret: unlike the StuG, it can engage off-axis without swinging the hull.

British trials on captured Tigers (including work that followed 131) recorded five successive hits on a ~40 × 50 cm plate at ~1,100 m. Treat first-round hit chance as excellent out to 1,500 m against a tank-sized target.

### Rounds

German handbook figures, **vs plate at 30° from vertical**:

| Round | Type | Mass | Muzzle vel. | 100 m | 500 m | 1,000 m | 1,500 m | 2,000 m | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pzgr. 39** | APCBC-HE | 10.2 kg | 773 m/s | 120 mm | **110 mm** | **99 mm** | 91 mm | 83 mm | Default AP. Small filler, still better after-armour than APCR |
| **Pzgr. 40** | APCR (tungsten) | 7.3 kg | 930 m/s | 171 mm | **156 mm** | **138 mm** | 123 mm | 110 mm | Rare in Africa. Poor post-pen. Against the heaviest targets only |
| **Gr. 39 HL** | HEAT | 7.65 kg | 600 m/s | **90 mm any range** | same | same | same | same | Low velocity, worse hit chance past 1,000 m |
| **Sprgr. 39** | HE | ~9 kg | ~600 m/s | — | — | — | — | — | Infantry, AT guns, buildings. Scale to 5,000 m |

Recommended load historically: **~50% Pzgr. 39 / ~50% HE**, plus a handful of Pzgr. 40 if any tungsten reached Tunisia.

**Practical kill envelope in a 1943 desert game**

- Churchill IV, Sherman 75, Valentine, Crusader, M3 Lee: frontally dead from 1,000–2,000 m depending on angle.
- 6-pdr AT gun in the open: the Tiger should kill it first if the crew sees it.
- Side shots from a 6-pdr at a few hundred metres: the Tiger can die. Model that, or 131’s own story is a lie.

RoF for a good crew: ~8–10 rounds/min in a static hull, slower on the move.

## Mobility

For a 57-tonne box it is not clumsy. Regenerative steering and wide tracks give a tighter turn than a Sherman. Fuel and breakdowns are the real limits.

| | |
|---|---|
| Max road (ungoverned, early) | **45.4 km/h** in 8th gear — not used in service |
| Practical road | **~38–40 km/h** |
| Cross-country | **~20–25 km/h** (crews often quote ~10 km/h on bad going) |
| Power / weight (HL 210) | ~11.4 PS/t |
| Road range | ~120–195 km depending on source / driving style |
| Off-road range | ~80–110 km |
| Fuel use | brutal — on the order of 5–7 L/km cross-country |
| Step | ~0.8 m |
| Trench | ~2.3–2.5 m |
| Ford | ~1.2–1.6 m (deep-wading kit existed on early Tigers; rarely useful in Tunisia) |
| Gradient | ~30–35° |
| Min turn radius | ~3.44 m |
| Ground pressure (combat tracks) | ~1.04 kg/cm² |

**Tunisia-specific**

- Feifel filters are mandatory in dust. Clogged filters = cooked HL 210.
- Combat tracks (725 mm) for sand; transport tracks to fit rail / landing craft.
- Many North African bridges will not take 57 t. Fording and bypasses matter.
- Petrol + rear radiators: fire if the engine deck is holed.
- Interleaved wheels pack with mud (less of a problem in dry Tunisia than in Russia) and are a maintenance tax.

March orders for Tiger battalions: ~30 km/h on road, 20–25 km/h on average ground. It can keep up with Panzer IIIs and IVs on a road move. It cannot sprint across a wadi all day.

## How it should play (Sudden Strike-style)

**Identity:** a rare, expensive king of the long shot. One or two on the map, not a platoon of ten.

Give it:

- Long-range first-hit probability that makes 75 mm tanks feel obsolete from the front
- Frontal bounce vs 6-pdr / 75 mm except on weak spots
- Full turret — it is allowed to engage a flanker without hull-spinning like a StuG
- Morale / suppression aura: Allied crews in 1943 *feared* Tigers more than the numbers justified

Force these costs or it flattens the Tunisia scenario:

- Very high cost / very low availability (s.Pz.Abt. 504 had a handful)
- Fuel and breakdown chance higher than a Panzer IV, worse in dust if Feifel is damaged
- 25 mm roof — artillery and air are the correct counters when you cannot get a 6-pdr on the side
- Turret ring and mantlet underside as real weak spots (this is how 131 was taken)
- Recovery: a knocked-out Tiger should be hard to retrieve; Germans often destroyed their own
- Width and weight: some bridges, streets and landing craft simply refuse it

**Do not** give 131 late-war toys: no *Zimmerit*, no steel wheels, no 40 mm roof, no HL 230, no periscope cupola. Those belong on a 1944 Normandy / East Front Tiger.

**Encyclopedia line**

> s.Pz.Abt. 504’s 131. Early Tiger I, chassis 250122, drum cupola, Feifel filters, 8.8 cm L/56. Fifty-seven tonnes that could kill any Allied tank in Tunisia from a kilometre. Stopped on 24 April 1943 by a 6-pounder that never pierced the front — it jammed the turret ring. The crew bailed. The tank still runs.

## Capture (for scenario / flavour text)

24 April 1943, Point 174. British infantry (2nd Sherwood Foresters) and supporting Churchills (including 48 RTR in the wider fight) take the hill. A 6-pdr solid shot strikes under 131’s 88, ricochets off the mantlet into the turret ring, jams traverse, wounds crew, kills the radio. Further hits on a turret lifting lug and the loader’s hatch. The crew abandon a still-drivable tank. Churchill and King George VI later inspect it in Tunisia. Shipped to the UK October 1943, evaluated at Chobham, now Bovington.

If you run a “capture the Tiger” mission, the win condition is *disable and hold*, not brew-up. A burned 131 is historically the wrong ending.
